What the game is for

Every card is a main-group element from periods 1–6. Each round you pick one property and the higher value wins (or the lower one, when the direction token says so). The numbers stay hidden until the reveal, so the only way to pick well is to reason from where the element sits on the periodic table. When the simple trend fails, the reveal says so and shows the real data.

Where the numbers come from

All values were generated once from the mendeleev Python package (version 1.3.0) and committed with the game; nothing is looked up while you play. Each dataset below is the one that package documents for the field used.

Why your textbook may disagree

Different books compile different measurements: covalent radii from different bond sets, ionization energies rounded differently, older electronegativity tables. The game only ever uses the order of two values, and the order almost never changes between compilations. Where it does, that is exactly the kind of pair the reveal flags as an upset.

Cells that say “undefined”

  • Atomic radius for the six noble gases. Noble-gas radii are measured on a different basis from the rest of the table and are not comparable, so the game refuses to rank them rather than teach an artifact. A card with no value cannot choose that stat, and loses the round if the other player does.
  • Electronegativity for He, Ne, Ar, Kr and Rn. These gases form no ordinary bonds, so the Pauling scale assigns them no value. Xenon does have a Pauling value in this dataset, from its few compounds with fluorine and oxygen, and keeps it.

How the reveal decides what to say

A simple predictor reads only each card's period and column and applies the textbook rules: radius rises going down and falls going right; ionization energy and electronegativity do the opposite; mass rises with atomic number; valence electrons equal the column. Then it compares its guess with the data.

  • Trend predicts and is right — the line names the rule and the columns or periods involved.
  • Trends conflict — one card is down-and-right or up-and-left of the other, so the two rules disagree. The line says so and lets the data decide.
  • Upset — the trend predicted the other card. The card gets an upset badge. A chemical reason appears only after the teacher has reviewed it; until then the line simply says the pair breaks the simple trend.

Mastery and privacy

The mastery panel and your settings are stored in this browser only (localStorage). Nothing is sent anywhere, and the game makes no network requests after the page loads. The “Copy results” line contains the first name you type and nothing else about you; it is meant for pasting into a Google Classroom question.

Standard

Massachusetts 2016 Science and Technology/Engineering Curriculum Framework, high school chemistry, HS-PS1-1 (framework p. 85):

Use the periodic table as a model to predict the relative properties of main group elements, including ionization energy and relative sizes of atoms and ions, based on the patterns of electrons in the outermost energy level of each element. Use the patterns of valence electron configurations, core charge, and Coulomb's law to explain and predict general trends in ionization energies, relative sizes of atoms and ions, and reactivity of pure elements.

State assessment boundary: limited to main group (s and p block) elements — which is exactly the Phase 1 deck.
