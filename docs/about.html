What the game is for

Every card is an element. The CP deck is the 42 main-group elements of periods 1–6; the Honors deck adds the ten 3d metals, scandium to zinc, for 52 cards. Each round you pick one property and the higher value wins (or the lower one, when the direction token says so). The numbers stay hidden until the reveal, so the only way to pick well is to reason from where the element sits on the periodic table. When the simple trend fails, the reveal says so and shows the real data.

Where the numbers come from

All values were generated once from the mendeleev Python package (version 1.3.0) and committed with the game; nothing is looked up while you play. Each dataset below is the one that package documents for the field used.

Why your textbook may disagree

Different books compile different measurements: covalent radii from different bond sets, ionization energies rounded differently, older electronegativity tables, ionic radii for a different coordination number. The game only ever uses the order of two values, and the order almost never changes between compilations. Where it does, that is exactly the kind of pair the reveal flags as an upset.

Cells that say “undefined”

  • Atomic radius for the six noble gases. Noble-gas radii are measured on a different basis from the rest of the table and are not comparable, so the game refuses to rank them rather than teach an artifact. A card with no value cannot choose that stat, and loses the round if the other player does.
  • Electronegativity for He, Ne, Ar, Kr and Rn. These gases form no ordinary bonds, so the Pauling scale assigns them no value. Xenon does have a Pauling value in this dataset, from its few compounds with fluorine and oxygen, and keeps it.
  • Electron affinity for He, Be, N, Ne, Mg, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn, Mn and Zn. None of these forms a stable negative ion. For six of them the dataset has no value. For He, Be, N, Ar and Xe it lists a negative number, which the package documentation says belongs to a short-lived (metastable) ion; comparing that with ordinary electron affinities would teach the wrong thing, so the game treats it as undefined.
  • Ionic radius for H, C, N, Si, P, As, Po, At and the six noble gases. These have no common simple ion with a six-coordinate radius in Shannon's table (nitride, N³⁻, is listed only for four neighbours).
  • Valence electrons for the 3d metals. “Valence electrons equal the column” is a main-group rule, and a d-block element has no main-group column.

How the reveal decides what to say

A simple predictor reads only each card's period and group and applies the textbook rules: radius rises going down and falls going right; ionization energy, electronegativity and electron affinity do the opposite; mass rises with atomic number; valence electrons equal the column. For ionic radius one rule comes first: an anion is larger than a cation. Then it compares its guess with the data.

  • Trend predicts and is right — the line names the rule and the columns (or, once a 3d metal is involved, the groups) or periods.
  • Trends conflict — one card is down-and-right or up-and-left of the other, so the two rules disagree. The line says so and lets the data decide.
  • Upset — the trend predicted the other card. The card gets an upset badge. A chemical reason appears only after the teacher has reviewed it; until then the line simply says the pair breaks the simple trend.

Why the 3d metals bend the trends

Across the 3d row each added electron goes into the inner 3d subshell, while the outer 4s electrons stay put (two of them, or one for chromium and copper, whose configurations [Ar] 3d5 4s and [Ar] 3d10 4s are printed on their cards). Inner d electrons shield the outer ones from much of each added proton, so the outer electrons feel only a slowly growing pull. In this game's data, first ionization energy rises only from 633 kJ/mol (Sc) to 906 kJ/mol (Zn) across ten elements, while across the six p-block elements from Al to Cl it more than doubles, from 578 to 1251 kJ/mol. With steps that small, other effects — pairing electrons, full and half-full subshells, and how the dataset treats spin states — often decide a pair, and the reveal calls it an upset. The effect reaches past the row, too: gallium's radius (122 pm) is no smaller than aluminum's (121 pm), even though gallium has an extra shell.

Mastery and privacy

The mastery panel and your settings are stored in this browser only (localStorage). Nothing is sent anywhere, and the game makes no network requests after the page loads. The “Copy results” line contains the first name you type and nothing else about you; it is meant for pasting into a Google Classroom question.

Standard

Massachusetts 2016 Science and Technology/Engineering Curriculum Framework, high school chemistry, HS-PS1-1 (framework p. 85):

Use the periodic table as a model to predict the relative properties of main group elements, including ionization energy and relative sizes of atoms and ions, based on the patterns of electrons in the outermost energy level of each element. Use the patterns of valence electron configurations, core charge, and Coulomb's law to explain and predict general trends in ionization energies, relative sizes of atoms and ions, and reactivity of pure elements.

State assessment boundary: limited to main group (s and p block) elements — which is exactly the CP deck. The Honors deck's 3d metals go beyond that boundary on purpose.
